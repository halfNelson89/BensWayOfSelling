Know their reality, ask better questions

Good questions come from knowing what the customer's day is like. When you understand what they are measured on and what a bad day looks like, your questions sound like curiosity instead of a pitch.

Everyone in the room has a different scoreboard

The surgeon, the OR director, the scrub team and value analysis all feel bleeding differently. Ask each one about the part of the day they own.

Shape the conversation; don't frame a problem

Problem framing means telling the customer what is wrong before they have said it. It puts them on defense, because their current way of working is a choice they made. Shaping means asking about how the work goes and letting the challenge surface in their words.

Framed
"What problems do you have with bleeding in your fusions?"
Shaped
"Walk me through the decompression on a typical two-level."
Framed
"Isn't turnover your biggest problem?"
Shaped
"What happens between your first and second case?"
Framed
"Why do you still use cottonoids?"
Shaped
"Tell me how your team settled on the setup you use now."

The open-question test

Could the customer disagree without feeling corrected? If not, rewrite it. Start with How, What or Tell me about. Avoid Why: it sounds like an accusation, even when you don't mean it to.

Four tools to keep them talking

The goal is listen 70, talk 30. These four tools from Chris Voss get you there without talking more.

Mirror

Repeat the last one to three words with a rising tone, then stay silent. It reliably produces two or three more sentences of detail.

Customer: "Then I'm packing and waiting."
You: "Packing and waiting?"

Label

Name what you observe. Always "It seems" or "It sounds like." Never "I think" or "What I'm hearing," which make it your opinion.

"It sounds like an implementation has gone badly here before."

Accusation audit

Say the negative thing they are already thinking before they say it. Most useful where there is a bad prior experience.

"You're probably thinking this is another rep who disappears after the install."

"That's right" checkpoint

Summarize what you heard before Align and listen for the exact words. Lesson 5 covers it in depth.

"That's right" means understood. "You're right" means please stop.
